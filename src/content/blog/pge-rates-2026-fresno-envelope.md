## Why your PG&E bill keeps climbing, and when it hurts most

Two things are happening at once.

First, PG&E residential rates have risen repeatedly through 2025 and 2026, and
more increases have been proposed for later years. We are not going to quote you
a precise cents-per-kilowatt-hour number as gospel, because it changes and it
depends on your plan. Pull up your own bill for the real figure.

Second, and this is the part most homeowners miss, most homes are now on a
time-of-use plan. On PG&E's most common plan, power costs the most during the
peak window, which runs late afternoon into the evening, commonly 4pm to 9pm.
That is not a coincidence problem you can shrug off in Fresno. Those are exactly
the hours a Valley home is hottest, when your attic has been baking all day and
your air conditioner is running flat out to keep up. So you are running your
biggest electrical load during the most expensive hours of the day. That is the
engine behind the bill.

The good news hiding in that sentence: if you can make your AC work less during
the 4pm to 9pm window, you are cutting the most expensive kilowatt-hours you buy.
That is where the home envelope comes in.

## The cheapest kilowatt-hour is the one you never use

Here is the framing a solar company has no reason to give you: before you
finance a system that generates power, it is almost always smarter to reduce the
power you waste. The cheapest kilowatt-hour is the one you never use.

Solar has its place. But a solar loan is a 20-year commitment sized to your
current, leaky, heat-soaked house. If you air seal and insulate first, your AC
pulls less, your peak demand drops, and any solar you add later can be smaller
and cheaper because it has less to cover. Doing it in the other order means you
pay to generate power you are still leaking out through the attic.

We do not sell solar, so we do not have a dog in that fight. We just see a lot of
Fresno homes that spent big on panels while a hot, under-insulated attic quietly
kept the AC grinding through every peak window. Fix the envelope, then decide.

## Walk the envelope in ROI order

For a hot Fresno home, the upgrades that beat the peak have a natural order.
Spend from the top down and each dollar does more.

![New synthetic underlayment and materials staged on a Fresno reroof](/photos/case-studies/farris/farris-03.jpg)

### 1. Air sealing and attic insulation (the biggest bang)

This is almost always the first dollar. Air sealing closes the gaps where
conditioned air leaks out and attic heat sneaks in, and attic insulation slows
the heat trying to push down into your living space. Together they take the
hardest edge off the afternoon heat load, which is the load that lands right in
the peak window.

ENERGY STAR estimates homeowners save about 15% on heating and cooling costs by
air sealing and adding attic insulation. That is an honest, national estimate,
not a guarantee about your specific house, and we will not inflate it. But it is
a real, repeatable result, and it usually costs a fraction of a financed solar
system. If you want the deeper explanation, we wrote a full piece on
[how proper insulation can reduce your energy costs](/blog/how-proper-insulation-can-reduce-energy-costs/),
and you can read about our [attic insulation](/insulation/attic-insulation/) and
broader [insulation services](/insulation/) too.

### 2. Radiant barrier for the attic heat load

A radiant barrier is a reflective layer in the attic that pushes back the
radiant heat pouring off a sun-baked roof deck. In our climate, attic
temperatures can climb brutally high on a summer afternoon, and that heat radiates
straight down onto your insulation and ceilings. A
[radiant barrier](/insulation/radiant-barrier/) cuts that radiant load, which
takes even more work off your AC during the peak.

It works best on top of good air sealing and insulation, not instead of them. If
your attic is the right candidate, it is one of the better second dollars in a
Valley home. We will tell you straight whether yours qualifies.

### 3. Energy-efficient windows (real comfort, usually after attic work)

Good [energy-efficient windows](/windows/) genuinely help. They cut solar heat
gain through the glass, kill drafts, and make a west-facing room that used to
bake in the afternoon actually livable. The comfort difference is real and
immediate.

But we will be honest with you: windows alone will not fix a hot house. If your
attic is leaking and thin on insulation, new windows are a nice upgrade sitting
on top of an unsolved problem. Per dollar, attic work almost always comes first,
then windows once the envelope above you is handled. We break down the tradeoffs
in [the benefits of energy-efficient windows](/blog/benefits-of-energy-efficient-windows/).

## How time-of-use pricing works, in plain English

Time-of-use just means the price of electricity changes depending on the hour.
Off-peak hours are cheaper. Peak hours, commonly 4pm to 9pm on PG&E's most common
plan, are the most expensive. Every kilowatt-hour your AC pulls in that window is
billed at the top rate.

Now picture a typical Valley summer day. The attic has soaked up heat since
morning. By late afternoon it is radiating that heat down into the house right as
the peak window opens, so the AC runs hardest during the priciest hours. A
tighter envelope changes that story in two ways. It reduces how much your AC has
to run at all, and it flattens the afternoon spike so less of your cooling lands
inside the expensive 4pm to 9pm window. You are not just using less power, you
are using less of the most expensive power.

## A worked example (use your own bill)

Let us make this concrete. This is an example to show the logic, not a promise
about your home. Always use your own bill for the real numbers.

Example, for illustration only:

- Say your summer peak rate is around 40 cents per kWh. Check your own bill for
  your actual rate and plan, it will differ.
- Say your cooling adds roughly 600 kWh in a hot summer month.
- ENERGY STAR estimates air sealing plus attic insulation saves about 15% on
  heating and cooling. Fifteen percent of 600 kWh is about 90 kWh less in that
  month.
- At the example rate of 40 cents, 90 kWh works out to roughly $36 in that month.

Now layer in time-of-use. The kilowatt-hours you shave are concentrated in the
hot afternoon, which is peak-priced, so the dollars you save skew toward your
most expensive hours. That is why a tighter envelope hits the bill harder than a
flat "15% off" makes it sound.

Run the same three lines with your own peak rate and your own summer usage and
you will see your own payback. If your rate is higher than the example (many are)
or your house is leakier than average, the number moves in your favor. If you
want a fast starting point on project cost, our
[instant estimate tool](/estimate/) gives you a range before anyone visits.

We are showing you the method, not selling you a savings figure. Any contractor
who promises you an exact dollar amount before measuring your attic is guessing.
